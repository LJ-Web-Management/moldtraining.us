State-by-State Guide

State Mold Training & Licensing Requirements

Most states have no mold-specific licensing program. A handful, including Florida, Texas, and New York, license mold assessors and remediators directly. Here's how to tell which rules apply to you.

Jump to State Rules See Our Courses

Short answer → There is no federal mold license. Whether you need one depends entirely on your state. Florida, Texas, and New York run dedicated mold assessor/remediator licensing programs; most other states do not, though local or industry-specific rules can still apply. Our courses provide training and a certificate of completion, and support state licensing preparation, but do not themselves satisfy a state license requirement.

States With Dedicated Mold Licensing

Florida, Texas & New York

These three states run the most established mold-specific licensing programs in the country. If you work in one of them professionally, state licensing controls.

Florida

Licenses Mold Assessors and Mold Remediators separately through the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR), including required education hours and a state exam.

Texas

Licenses mold assessors, remediation contractors, and remediation workers through the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR) under the Texas Mold Assessment and Remediation Rules.

New York

Licenses mold assessors and mold remediation contractors under Labor Law Article 32, administered by the New York State Department of Labor (NYSDOL).

Licensing details, fees, and continuing-education rules change. Always confirm current requirements directly with the relevant state agency before relying on this summary.

Everywhere Else

Most States Have No Mold-Specific License

Outside Florida, Texas, and New York, most states do not run a dedicated mold assessor or remediator license. That doesn't mean mold work is unregulated everywhere else, general contractor licensing, environmental rules, or local health department requirements can still apply depending on the job and jurisdiction. When in doubt, check with your state's contractor licensing board or environmental agency.
